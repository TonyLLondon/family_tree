# Named in the Gazette

## A foreign editor, a Persian newspaper

From 1851 Edward Burgess edited the *Ruznāme-ye Vaqāye-e Ettefāqiye* — Iran’s first state newspaper — and translated for the court. His letters complain of the printer and tread carefully around foreign news; Lady Sheil, wife of the British minister in Tehran, saw the same man juggling public gazette and confidential bulletin for the Shah. Week after week the lithographed columns went out without naming him. Then, in **late January 1854**, **issue 156** of the National Library facsimile run broke that silence.

## Summer 1853 — the capital empties

Issue **131** is dated **Thursday 28 Shawwāl 1269** — **4 August 1853**. On the same front page the domestic news speaks of **ناخوشی** (sickness) and of people leaving Tehran in fear. Life at court went on, but European letters from the same months describe cholera and panic across the country. Edward was translating, censoring, and chasing Hadji Abdul Mahomed for copy while the city thinned around him.

## “Confined to his bed by fever”

On **30 October 1853** a correspondent in Tehran sent home a dispatch that reached London in mid-December. The *Evening Standard* quoted it at length under **Persia**. One sentence names **Mr. Burgess**, English editor of the government gazette, **confined to his bed by fever** while **a Persian ignoramus** published an article that offended British readers. Edward’s own letters had already warned that a substitute could blunder into diplomacy; here was the proof in print.

## Issue 156 — **برجیس صاحب** in court news

Count **twenty-five weekly issues** from August to January and the masthead shows **156**: **Thursday 28 Rabīʿ al-Thānī 1270**, the publication week of **about 26 January 1854**. Page two carries court news. The ministers report that the services of **برجیس صاحب** — **مباشر روزنامه** (overseer of the gazette) and **مترجم دیوان** (court translator) — have been found acceptable, **especially** in a time of **ناخوشی و وبا** when most people had fled to the **یلاقات** (summer quarters). He stayed in **دارالخلافه**, kept the paper going, and from the royal side received **خلعت**: **one roll of Kashmiri termeh shawl**.

## What the shawl signified

**خلعت** was how the Qajar court said thank you in public: favour you could see, and often sell. Kashmiri shawls — fine pashmina, woven in Kashmir, traded through India — had become a prized grade of that gift by the 1840s. A **طاقه**, a bolt rolled for storage, could dress a household or change hands in the bazaar. Printing the award in the gazette put Edward’s loyalty on the same page as appointments and decrees — visible to any subscriber in the capital.

## London, Tehran, one winter

The Standard’s **fever** line belongs to **October 1853**. The lithograph’s **وبا** and **خلعت** belong to **January 1854**. Different languages, different audiences, the same man at the same desk through a season when the capital emptied and the paper still had to come out Thursday after Thursday.

## February 1855 — “not secure until published in the Gazette”

By **1855** Edward’s spine and limbs were failing. He petitioned for **leave to England** — the home he had not seen since **1831**. The Prime Minister agreed in conversation, but Edward told his father Henry on **26 February 1855** that the arrangement was **not secure until it appeared in the gazette**. He planned Tehran → Tabriz → Trebizond → Constantinople → Trieste → England, and wrote with gratitude for **“so good and kind a father.”**

## Issue ~219 — sick leave to London

Roughly **sixty-three weekly issues** after **156**, the court bulletins on **NLIR vol. 2, PDF p. 581** name him again. **برجیس صاحب**, **مترجم** and **مباشر** of the newspaper, had been **مریض و علیل**; physicians wanted a **change of climate**; **father and relatives** had petitioned; **اذن مریض** came **by royal command**, and he was **to be sent to England — London**. For the first time in a quarter-century the **state paper he edited** recorded permission to go home. His **14 March** letter to George still speaks of packing and mules — bureaucracy and illness moving at different speeds.

## The road that did not reach England

The gazette notice is brisk and bureaucratic. What followed was not a simple voyage. Anna remembered **six months’ leave**, a **takhteravan** litter, **nearly three months** from Tehran toward Tabriz, **Dr Cormick** at the end, and death **about 12 June 1855** — **three days after Fanny’s first birthday**. Edward was buried in the **Armenian cemetery outside Tabriz**, a few yards from her uncle. The lithograph gave him **London**; the journey gave him **Tabriz**.
